Instant Insanity on a quantum computer1 / 7
Instant Insanity is a 1960s toy: four cubes with coloured faces, and you must stack them so each of the four long sides shows all four colours once. There are 331,776 ways to stack them and only 8 work, which are really one tower seen in 8 rotated or flipped positions. We asked how well a small quantum search, on 12 qubits, can find that tower, and what it costs.

Instant Insanity on a quantum computer3 / 7
We write each cube's position, a number from 0 to 5, in 3 qubits, so 4 cubes need 12 qubits instead of the starter's 24. Codes 6 and 7 are not placements and our circuits never produce them. A random placement of every cube finds the tower 0.15 % of the time (0.049 % for any 12 random bits), and before any quantum run we checked that our cost function is zero only on the two answers.

Two quantum searches, side by side

QAOA

A round = score each combination by the rules it breaks, then shuffle each cube among its 6 placements.
Generalizes: the same angles work for 5 to 8 cubes.

Grover

A step = a checker built only from the rules marks the solutions, then an amplifier boosts them.
Wins for 4 cubes: fewer gates, higher success.

Instant Insanity on a quantum computer4 / 7
QAOA repeats rounds: a cost step that marks each combination by how many rules it breaks, then a mixer that shuffles each cube only between its 6 possible placements; 24 rounds reach 98.5 %, and its angles were trained classically on the 4-cube puzzle. Grover repeats steps: a checker generated from the rules alone flips the sign of solved towers and an amplifier boosts them; 19 steps reach 99.85 %, and one idle helper qubit cut its circuit from 25,666 to 3,987 two-qubit gates. Grover needs to know there are 2 answers, which we get from a classical count.

Instant Insanity on a quantum computer6 / 7
On a perfect simulator and on qBraid's SV1 cloud simulator the deep circuits find the tower almost every time: Grover 99.95 % and QAOA 98.6 % on SV1, and 46.6 % for the 5-cube bonus puzzle, against random-guess rates of 0.15 % and 0.026 %. On the real Rigetti quantum computer we could only run one-step Grover circuits of about 250 two-qubit gates, and they gave zero correct towers out of 500: the noise erases the signal. Deeper circuits were rejected by the QPU.

Bonus: 4 to 8 cubes with the same angles

Still works: 17 to 48 % success at 5 to 8 cubes, with no retraining.
Grows: 3 qubits and 5,600 to 10,600 two-qubit gates per extra cube.
Honest: brute force is still faster. No speedup claimed.
Next: quantum counting, so Grover finds the number of answers itself.
Instant Insanity on a quantum computer7 / 7
For the bonus we generated 5 to 8 cube puzzles with the starter's make_puzzle and seeds 16, 86, 68 and 4, each with exactly one tower. We did not retrain: the 24-round angles from the 4-cube puzzle still give 17 to 48 % exact success, while a random guess drops to about one in a million at 8 cubes. A classical brute force is still much faster at these sizes, so this shows the method transfers, not that it is faster. Next we would add quantum counting, so Grover finds the number of answers itself instead of taking it from a classical count; thank you.
